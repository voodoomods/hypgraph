Don't believe the hype.Test the hyp.

HypGraph is a research record that can't flatter itself. You write down hypotheses, experiments, results and claims. It works out — every time you look — what was reviewed, what rests on something that fell, and how many independent lines of evidence there really are. It refuses the claims that hype is made of.

Refusal is a feature

Watch a claim get refused.

A student measures two trays of cress. The first claim says "always" about one week of two trays. The second invents a number. The third binds every number to the result it came from — and only then does it enter the record, unchecked, until a reviewer who isn't its author checks it.

  • every number in a claim must match a result field
  • "always", "never", "proves" need k of n with k = 0 or n
  • "rules out" needs a search shown to find a planted answer

Eight rules

    
Every rule is a mistake that was really made.

    
HypGraph came out of a team of AI agents doing long investigations, and the errors they made despite
      instructions not to. Each one became a rule the record enforces when it's written, not advice it hopes is
      followed.

    

      
Standing is derived
No field says "confirmed". Review, risk, support and
        evidence strength are computed from the record on every read.

      
Nothing is rewritten
Append-only, SHA-256 hash-chained. Corrections are new
        versions; withdrawals stay on record. verify() catches any edit.

      
Reviews pin versions
Revise a checked claim and its check goes stale. A
        decision based on the old version is flagged to revisit.

      
Claims carry numbers
Every number bound to a result field. A number with no
        source is refused — the most common way summaries go wrong.

      
Independence is counted
Three results from one run are one line of evidence,
        not three. Lines that share data or a source merge.

      
Exclusions need power
"Rules out" only after a search was shown to find a
        planted answer. Otherwise it's inconclusive.

      
Nobody reviews their own
Only named reviewers' verdicts count, never on their
        own work. Everyone else's are kept as comments.

      
Instruments speak for themselves
Only the lab runner files a lab-recorded run.
        A run you describe is labelled as your report.

    

  






  

    
Who it's for

    
From a classroom to a lab to a team of agents.

    

      
students
A notebook that asks "how do you know?"
At the moment
        you write the claim, not when it's marked. Your record is something a teacher can verify.

      
teachers
Grade the method
See what each student predicted before
        they measured, what they claimed, and what the evidence supports. Ships with a classroom case.

      
researchers
Publish the record with the paper
One bundle a
        referee can verify: every claim bound to its numbers, every run hashed, every review pinned.

      
AI agents
Guardrails that explain themselves
An MCP server whose
        refusals say exactly what to fix — so an agent's research gets better, not just louder.

    

  






  

    

      
Trust, but verify

      
Check anyone's record in your browser.

      
A bundle carries every event as the exact string that was hashed. Verifying it is SHA-256 over
        strings — no trust in us, our code or the sender. Drop one in and it opens in the Atlas.
