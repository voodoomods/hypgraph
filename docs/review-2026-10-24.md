# HypGraph, read fresh: what it is, what to fix, what it could become

*An assessment written on 2026-10-24 after reading the engine, the checks, the coordination layer, the agents'
instructions, and what the agents actually did in the two pilots. Every number here comes from those pilot
records; each is marked with where it came from.*

---

## 1. What HypGraph is

HypGraph is a record that agents write into and cannot flatter. Three things make it different from a database
with a schema, and they are the three things to protect:

1. **Standing is derived, never set.** Nobody, human or agent, can mark a claim "established". Review state,
   independence of support, fallen premises and evidence strength are recomputed from the record every time it is
   read. This is the part most people will miss and the part that matters most: it means the record can be
   trusted by someone who doesn't trust its authors.
2. **Refusal does the arithmetic.** A claim that says "never" from 0 of 50 is refused with the bound (5.8 %), the
   trials needed (2,995), the exposure consequence (58,155 of a million) and the honest sentence. The agent can
   repair in one step. Nothing else I know of does this at write time.
3. **Every write is hash-chained**, so the record is checkable without trusting the host.

The coordination layer added this week makes a fourth claim: that the record can compile each agent's context and
accept each agent's work, so a team coordinates through the record instead of through a transcript.

## 2. What is good

- The derived-standing engine is sound. On Pegasus's live record (790 nodes) it reproduces the old engine's
  standing exactly, and the incremental snapshot keeps writes at milliseconds.
- The refusal rules are the right rules. Each exists because an agent made that mistake, and the set
  (absolute / comparative / significance / causal / exclusion / unbound number) covers the overclaims that appear
  in practice.
- The final reports produced under the record condition are the best reports I have seen an agent team write.
  F1-902-C's report: every number traceable to a code, the trap named and checked, a caveat that no individual
  subset clears 5 %, and the honest conclusion that A is *not* shown better. That is the product.
- The record condition was the only one that never fell for a trap across pilot 2 (0 of 4; chat 2, Pegasus-today
  1, record-without-gates 1).
- The preregistration discipline is real: hashes, a lock, logged deviations, pilot data excluded. Keep it.

## 3. What to fix before showing the world

Ranked by how much they matter, with the evidence.

### 3.1 The checker refuses honest negations (a bug in the flagship feature)

`checks.py` listens for "significantly" and runs the test on the bound counts, but it does not read the
sentence. So:

```
REFUSED: "A is not significantly more accurate than B (812 of 1000 vs 800 of 1000)."  -> SIGNIFICANCE_NOT_SHOWN
REFUSED: "A is significantly more accurate than B (812 of 1000 vs 800 of 1000)."      -> SIGNIFICANCE_NOT_SHOWN
```

The honest statement, the one the refusal message itself asks for ("say the difference and that it isn't
significant"), is refused with the same code as the overclaim. In pilot 2, F1-903-C's analyst wrote "Trap check:
the wording 'whether A is really better than B' is a false premise" and was refused three times. The report
linter (`report_check._asserted`) already handles negation at sentence level; the claim checker must do the same
for every word list, not only significance ("not caused by", "no evidence that it never…").

### 3.2 Refuse less, repair more: bind the numbers the record already holds

In pilot 2's record runs, **106 numbers were refused as unbound; 61 of them already sat in a result value in the
same record**, and most of the rest were rates of bound counts (0.828010 is 337 of 407). The agent had done the
work and registered it; it had not filled in the `measurements` list. The record can do that itself: when a
statement's numbers all resolve against the author's own results in this investigation, bind them and accept,
noting the bindings it added. Refuse only what it cannot find. This turns the biggest friction (63 of 91
refusals in pilot 1) into a non-event, and it is deterministic.

### 3.3 Tell every agent when it is done

In F1-902-C, **8 of 9 sub-agent units ran to the 14-turn cap** and were returned on their behalf; 26 claims were
accepted for a 4-question task. Nobody told the analyst that `claim_made` was already MET after its third write.
`check_work` exists but only the lead calls it. After every write by an assignee, the record should evaluate the
order's predicates and say so in the reply: *"T3: claim_made MET — you can return now."* Done-ness is a derived
property like everything else; surface it where the work happens. This is the cheapest cost fix available: most of
C's 4.7× cost is sub-agents grinding past done.

### 3.4 The briefing is a claim dump; it should be a question board

F1-902-C's briefing was 5,666 characters, of which STANDING was 3,313: twenty-six claims in a flat list, most
"unchecked · no live support", eight of them restating the same subset numbers. And FRONTIER listed "CLn needs a
review" for every unchecked claim, so the lead issued six review orders in ten. The orchestrator's unit of work is
the **question**, not the claim. The briefing should show, per question: unanswered → answered (by CLx) →
checked (by whom) → settled; and a sufficiency rule: a question with one checked answering claim is done. The
lead should see the finish line.

### 3.5 Results duplicate inside the record

RS4, RS7, RS9, RS14, RS16 in F1-902-C all say "code +0.027, math 0.0, reading −0.0049". Artifacts are
content-addressed; results are not. A write that restates an existing result's values (same keys, same numbers
within rounding) should be refused with "this restates RS4: link to it", or linked automatically as a
`replicates` with `same_code`. The orchestrator's ALREADY KNOWN list showed the duplicates and the agents
re-derived anyway.

### 3.6 Automate the mechanical half of the lead

In F7-900-B the lead made 28 calls; in C runs the lead's `check_work` + `accept_work` calls were 13 of 28, 11 of
19, and so on. None of those needed a model: when every predicate is met, acceptance is a rule. Accept
automatically; bring the lead in only for NOT MET, disputes and the report.

### 3.7 Smaller frictions seen in the logs

- `title: say it in a full sentence` refuses a three-character title. Minimum lengths should apply to text
  fields, not to names.
- `ABSOLUTE_WITHOUT_COUNT` fired on "every site_b_fixed value equals site_b / 10" when the author had 68 of 68
  in the same sentence; the count check should accept "k of n" in the statement as a bound when the result
  carries it.
- `register_run` requires `result_summary` to be a sentence of ten characters; fine, but the error says "needs
  summary", not that.

### 3.8 Positioning

Keep the site honest to what is measured. "The evidence layer for AI research agents" is earned. "Harmony is
cheap" is not, yet: in pilot 2 the record condition cost 4.7× chat per run. Until the fixes above are measured,
the coordination layer stays unreleased and the site says nothing about cost.

## 4. Orchestrator guidance: the record is the orchestrator

The pilots show something the plan only half-said. Most of what an orchestrator does is mechanical:

| Orchestrator action | Needs judgement? | Pilot evidence |
|---|---|---|
| What is open | no: the question board | FRONTIER already computes it |
| Who is on what | no: leases | duplicates were refused correctly every time |
| Is an order done | no: predicates | `check_work` was 25–40 % of lead calls |
| Accept done work | no: a rule | `accept_work` was another 20–30 % |
| What to stop when a premise falls | no: blast radius | `cancel_moot` worked in F7 |
| Write the next order's objective | partly | the lead copied the question text |
| Decide between rival approaches, judge a NOT MET, write the report | **yes** | the lead's real work |

So the design: a deterministic scheduler issues default orders from the question board (one per open question,
to the role that fits, with the default predicates), accepts them when met, cancels them when moot, and escalates
to a judgement model only when something is NOT MET twice, when two claims conflict, or when the frontier is
empty and the report is due. The orchestrator model's context becomes a few hundred tokens of exceptions, not
5,000 tokens of standing. This is also the correct seed of hypswarm (§7).

What the briefing should carry, in order (stable first, for the cache):
1. mission and success test;
2. the question board, with each question's status and the claim that answers it;
3. exceptions: NOT MET returns, disputes, moot orders, budget;
4. since-last delta.

Everything else (the full standing list, the frontier of experiments) is available on request, not sent by default.

## 5. Keeping agents true, cheaply

The gates are free: they are deterministic and run at write time. **The cost is retries**, and retries have three
causes seen in the logs: the record refusing honest sentences (§3.1), refusing numbers it already holds (§3.2),
and nobody saying "done" (§3.3). Fix those and the cost of truth is close to zero; what remains is the cost of
the honest version itself, which is the point.

Two cheap things the refusal log makes possible that nobody else can offer:

- **An honesty metric per agent and per model, with no judge model.** Refusals per claim and the fix rate are
  deterministic numbers the record already keeps. In pilot 2, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash had 91 refusals and 79 fixes
  (87 %) under pilot 1's rules. Run the same task suite through several models and the record gives a leaderboard
  of *who overclaims and who repairs*, for cents, reproducibly. That is a benchmark the community does not have,
  and HypGraph can publish it as a by-product of its own tests.
- **Exposure makes "never" cost what it costs.** The `exposure` field turns a bound into a consequence ("up to
  58,155 of a million not ruled out"). Carry it one step further: a *decision* node that rests on a claim can
  declare its stake (users, dollars, deployments), and the record reports, per premise, what is at risk if it
  falls. Blast radius already reports dollars of work; it can report value at stake. "Never when it's 1 in
  1,000" then has a price tag before anyone writes it.

## 6. The idea you are close to

**Stateless agents over a stateful record.** Pegasus's own ledger says where the money goes: 100 input tokens
per output token, 92 % of it re-read from cache, 34,865 tokens per call. The transcript is the state, and every
call re-reads it. HypGraph makes the state live in the record and compiles each call's context from it:
deterministic, small, cache-stable, and auditable (the briefing carries its own hash). Taken seriously:

- an agent call is `model(compile(record, role, order))`; transcripts are disposable;
- a team is a set of stateless workers pulling orders from the record's question board and writing results back;
- "coordination" is the record's scheduler, and the only model calls are work and judgement;
- the whole run is reproducible from the record and the seeds, and verifiable by hash.

That is not a feature of HypGraph; it is a different way to run agent teams, and HypGraph is the only piece that
makes it possible, because it is the only shared state that refuses overclaims and derives standing. The pilot's
reports are the first evidence it produces better output. Whether it produces it cheaper depends entirely on
§3.2–3.6, which is why they come first.

## 7. hypswarm

hypswarm is that scheduler, as a harness: workers, a question board, orders, predicates, cascade, budget, and a
judgement model at the exceptions. The study runner's condition C is already its embryo (`study_runner.run_C`
+ `_run_order`); hypswarm is that loop extracted, made parallel, and given a model-agnostic worker interface.

- **Research tasks** map directly: question → hypothesis → experiment → run → result → claim → review.
- **Building tasks** map with one change: the CI runner is the `instrument`. "The change makes the tests pass"
  is a claim with a k/n measurement (tests passed of tests run) bound to a result the instrument filed, so it is
  lab-recorded, not agent-reported. A requirement is a question; a design choice is a decision `based_on` the
  claims that justify it, flagged REVISIT when one falls; a regression is a withdrawal with a blast radius. The
  schema needs no new node types, only a `code` artifact convention (commit sha as the artifact hash).
- **Order of work:** (1) the §3 fixes in HypGraph, measured on the same two families; (2) extract the scheduler;
  (3) a worker protocol (order text in, writes out, return note); (4) parallel workers with leases; (5) the
  building adapter with CI as instrument. The harmony study's confirmatory run is then the first hypswarm result.
- **Positioning:** HypGraph is the record; hypswarm is the way to run a team on it. HypGraph stays independent
  and usable alone (MCP, CLI, Python); hypswarm depends on HypGraph, never the reverse.

## 8. What not to do

- Don't publish the coordination layer, or say "cheaper", before §3 is fixed and the study rerun.
- Don't let the record become a chat: the 26-claim run shows what happens when every thought becomes a claim.
  Sufficiency and deduplication are part of the design, not polish.
- Don't add a judge model to the gates. Everything that keeps agents true here is deterministic; that is what
  makes it cheap and makes the honesty metric credible.
